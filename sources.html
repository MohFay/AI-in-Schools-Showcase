Additional context

  • Stanford SIEPR’s analysis says AI’s effect on overall employment is likely small in currently available evidence, while noting the difficult job market for new graduates may be partly connected to AI.
  • The same brief reports a 5.6% unemployment rate among recent graduates in early 2026. That number describes the labor market; it does not prove AI caused that rate.
  • Generative AI can help less-experienced workers complete some tasks more effectively. That does not remove the need to learn the underlying skills or check the output.
  • Jobs involving physical intervention, care, empathy, and complex real-world interaction may be harder to automate fully than routine information-processing tasks. This is a broad pattern, not a promise that any occupation is immune.
  • Customer service, data entry, and routine office tasks are exposed to automation. Exposure does not always mean a whole job disappears; roles and tasks can change.
  • Creative and complex cognitive work can also be affected. Human judgment, originality, context, ethical choices, and storytelling remain important parts of the work.
  • The BLS projects office and administrative support employment to decline by about 752,100 jobs from 2025 to 2035. The release discusses automation, including AI, as a factor; it does not attribute every projected loss to AI alone.
How to read the numbersEmployment projections estimate a possible future based on assumptions and available data. They are not guarantees, and they should not be treated as direct measurements of how many jobs AI itself will eliminate. The 5.6% figure is a recent-graduate unemployment estimate from Stanford SIEPR, not a BLS employment projection.
